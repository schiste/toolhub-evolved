# Technical and Marketing Notes

- The ToolsDB grant is 20 connections for the whole tool account, and every process was sized from one pair of numbers that described a webservice worker: `pool_size=2, max_overflow=2`. Four uwsgi workers therefore budgeted 16 of the 20 for themselves and left four for every scheduled job together, while each job process — running the identical `db.configure()` through `job_runner` — could take four of its own. Five job processes is an ordinary minute (one continuous worker, two once-a-minute jobs, a scheduled one or two overlapping), so routine demand was 36 against a limit of 20. It surfaced as `(1203, "User s57731 already has more than 'max_user_connections' active connections")` on 212 occasions and 107 HTTP 500s between 03:05 and 13:06 on 2026-09-02, entirely on `/v1/tools/summaries/` and `/v1/catalog/search/tools/` — the two endpoints the frontend requests in parallel. Nothing in the job logs reported it: every `job_guard` failure streak was zero throughout.
- `configure()` now takes `web=` and `concurrency=` and defaults to the job budget, so a call site added later and left undeclared costs two connections rather than four. Two is the floor rather than a preference: `advisory_lock()` holds a connection of its own for as long as its body runs and the body then opens a session, so the six lock-taking jobs given a single connection would not fail loudly — they would wait out `pool_timeout` and report a timeout. `projection_refresh` is the one job that runs work in parallel, and its `ThreadPoolExecutor` width and its pool now come from one `PARALLEL_SYNC_WORKERS` constant with a test that fails if they drift.
- The webservice ceiling dropped to `1 + 1`, taking the fleet from 16 to 8 and routine demand to 18 of 20. The budget is now asserted rather than described: `TOOLSDB_ACCOUNT_CONNECTION_LIMIT` and `WEBSERVICE_WORKERS` are named constants, and two tests require that the fleet claim at most half the account and that the fleet plus five job processes fit inside it. Both were checked against the previous values and fail there — `2 + 2` gives 26 and even `2 + 1` gives 22 — so neither passes vacuously. The trade is real and recorded in the source with the measurement behind it: 28.8% of requests took over a second on 2026-09-02 and the slowest took 28.9s, so one request that slow can hold a worker's connection past `POOL_TIMEOUT_SECONDS` and make its neighbors fail waiting. A queue timeout is bounded, local to one worker and attributable, where exhausting the account failed requests at random across the fleet — but the trade only stays sound while that tail is short.
- That slowest request was `/v1/catalog/search/facets/?include_archived=1`, and it is the third change. One global facet aggregate was published, built over `default_population()`, so `_has_catalog_filters()` reported `include_archived` as a departure from it and every archived-inclusive request counted facets live over the widest population there is. The predicate was not wrong; it answered whether the request departs from _the_ cache rather than which published aggregate describes it. There are now two published aggregates, rebuilt in one pass under a shared `generation` and `builtAt`, and `include_archived` selects between them. Measured against production: the default aggregate takes 4.23s over 8,995 rows and the complete one 11.05s over 14,778, so the background rebuild goes from 4.23s to 15.28s inside a job budgeted 300s while the request path sheds the whole cost. The query itself was already tuned — the `field, value, tool_name` index exists for it and its comment records the filesort it removed — so the remaining cost was the size of the population, not the plan. The rebuild treats a _missing_ payload as work rather than waiting for a dirty marker, because the new key does not exist on the first pass after this deploys and nothing marks the catalog dirty to announce it; a dirty-only trigger would leave it unpublished until something happened to edit the catalog and preserve the bug silently in the meantime.
- The site had no `robots.txt`, and almost none of its traffic is people. Over 2026-09-01, 27,276 requests carried 8,378 page loads across 6,754 distinct URLs — a repeat ratio of 1.24, where a reader revisits and a crawler does not. 531 were `/tools/:name/edit` and 509 `/tools/:name/history`, which nobody clicks on hundreds of tools they do not maintain, and the busiest hours were 03:00-08:00 UTC at roughly 2,000 requests an hour with no diurnal curve. Against 53,271 tools each answering at three routes, that is a systematic walk, and every page load fans out into several database-backed API calls — the same connections the three changes above had to make room for. The file withholds `/edit`, `/history`, `/search`, `?q=` and `/v1/`, keeps tool pages, author pages and the home page open, and sets a `Crawl-delay` for the crawlers that honour it. Wildcards rather than prefixes, because a tool's name sits in the middle of the path and no prefix rule can express "any tool's edit page". One caveat worth stating: uwsgi does not log user agents, so the automated share is inferred from request shape rather than identity — logging the user-agent header would settle it in a day.
- Validation: 3,603 proxy tests pass with 25 skipped, `ruff check` and `ruff format` clean, cspell clean. The facet timings above were measured with a read-only job against the production database rather than estimated, because the change triples the cost of a rebuild that runs inside a job timeout. The `robots.txt` tests were re-run after `tools/build_dist.py` so they exercise `dist/`, which is what production serves — the build copies and pre-compresses the file with no change needed — and one of them asserts the response is `text/plain` rather than merely a 200, since the clean-route fallback answers `index.html` for any unmatched path and a crawler handed HTML under `/robots.txt` reads it as no rules at all.
